# Trial Lab: State of Ohio v. Caleb Flynn

A vertical, interactive timeline that walks high-school students through the procedure of a real jury trial, using the Miami County, Ohio trial of Caleb Flynn. Students act as jurors: they learn each step, watch a focused clip, type responses, and reach their own verdict **before** the site reveals what the jury decided.

It is built for **five class periods of about 90 minutes**, delivered as back-and-forth instruction (teacher guidance, student typing). A student who misses a day can switch to **On my own** and complete it individually.

> **Note for the teacher:** this file contains lesson timings and notes. If you publish the folder in a public GitHub repository and do not want students to read it, delete `README.md` before you upload. The site itself does not need it.

## What is in the folder

- `index.html` is the entire site (no libraries, no outside files)
- `screenshots/` shows what each part looks like
- `.nojekyll` tells GitHub Pages to publish the files as they are

## How it works

1. **Cover.** The student types a name and chooses *With my class* or *On my own*. The mode can be changed at any time from the bar at the top. Students on a new device can load saved progress from the cover.
2. **Vertical timeline.** Five color-coded days and 20 stations, with a jump bar for each day. Cards stay small (icon, title, minutes, number of clips and questions).
3. **Each station has up to three steps:** Learn (key ideas and Ohio rules), Watch (a clip with a stated time window and “watch for” prompts), Respond (typed responses with a word counter and hints).
4. **Two ways to work.**
   - *With my class:* the longer explanations are collapsed because the teacher is covering them. Required responses are the questions from your assignments plus new ones. Discussion prompts are optional notes.
   - *On my own:* the full explanations are open, and discussion prompts become required typed responses.
5. **Verdict and reveal.** Station 19 asks for a vote on each charge and a written explanation. Station 20, with the jury’s verdict and sentence, stays locked until the student locks in their verdict. Nothing before it names the outcome.
6. **Word notebooks.** *Download Day N notebook* appears in each day’s header and in *My notebook*, and there is a complete notebook for all five days. A completion code is added at the end of each. Nothing is sent anywhere; the document is built in the browser.
7. **Courtroom guide.** A drawer with who’s who, the order of a jury trial (each step links to its station), key terms, and the charges.

Progress saves automatically in the student’s browser. It is tied to the device and web address, so a student should use the same computer to continue, or move it to another device with a progress file or code (see *Moving progress to another device*).

## The Alleigha Botner stations (Days 3 and 4)

Days 3 and 4 are devoted entirely to Botner. Four stations follow her testimony. They follow the pattern of your assignments (a goal, clips, and headed question groups).

- **Day 3, Station 12: how they met (the start of her direct).** How the prosecutor opens a direct examination, how she says she and Flynn met at the church in 2022, how the relationship changed, and the details the state used to show secrecy: the matching Harry Potter lightning-bolt tattoos (his hidden from his wife), meetings at her apartment and in his truck in a Vandalia Chipotle parking lot, and trips to other cities “to be away from people who would know us.” A question applies Ohio Evid.R. 403: are these details useful to the jury, or only embarrassing? Part 3 of its video (14:00 to 25:00) is the added “Also watch” window on how the relationship evolved.
- **Day 3, Station 13: the texts, nature of their relationship.** A color-coded timeline of messages in three phases (beginning, middle, end, including the January 11 breakup message, the cruise voicemail described in the text, and the September 29, 2025 message she apologized for reading). Five clips: early testimony, Botner crying in court as she read her own July 10, 2025 message, two windows (35:00 to 50:00 and 1:10:00 to 1:20:00) from a long video of her reading messages, chosen to show the nature of the relationship, and a 13-minute window (3:03:00 to 3:16:00) from Court TV’s full direct testimony, chosen to show how the two of them talked about Ashley and wanted her gone. Eight questions.
- **Day 4, Station 14: the night before and the morning after.** A timeline of February 14, February 15, after midnight, and the morning of February 16. A highlighted box, **“Her suspicion the next day: ‘too much of a coincidence’,”** gathers what she said about why she was suspicious (her pounding heart, being “very suspicious,” it seemed too much of a coincidence that Ashley was dead on the day the divorce was supposed to start) and how her view changed from her first FBI interview (she lied about the affair and said she did not believe he did it) to a later interview (she told the FBI she suspected him) to her testimony. It also covers what she did afterward (deleting messages, the lie, the searches) and the detective who located her. Five clips. Seven questions.
- **Day 4, Station 15: cross-examination by Emily Smith.** Bias and motive, her admission that she lied in her first FBI interview, her online searches, and how the defense reframed Flynn’s messages. A discussion question asks students to compare this cross with the officer’s and the sister’s.

The site keeps sexual content to a minimum (“intimate,” “relationship became sexual”). The texts about wanting Ashley dead are shown because they are the evidence the state relied on, shortened and with every quotation under 15 words. Her testimony ran almost seven hours over September 23 and 24, so the clips are short segments.

**About the “beginning.”** The earliest messages read aloud in court date from early 2025, about nine months after the relationship became sexual. The “beginning” phase therefore combines her testimony about how it started (“He would ask to come over just to hug me”) with the earliest messages the jury heard.

## The police theory station (Day 5)

**Station 17: from the last text to the 911 call.** Near the end of the state’s case, BCI criminal intelligence analyst Samantha Reeb walked jurors through timelines that compared Flynn’s account of the night with what his phone, Apple Watch, and AirPods recorded. The station shows a color-coded, minute-by-minute timeline (messages, device data, Flynn’s account, the 911 call, police, and what was never found) and four “How long?” cards: last message to the 911 call (12:45 to about 2:31 a.m., **1 hour 46 minutes**), last message to the first officer (about 2:36 a.m., **1 hour 51 minutes**), the 911 call to the drone search (about 3:50 a.m., about **1 hour 20 minutes**), and the most active watch burst to the call (3 minutes). The station explains the state’s theory that this window was long enough to shoot Ashley, stage the door, and get rid of the gun (which the state did not have to locate), and the defense’s answers (nothing showed he left the house, no blood or gunshot residue test, the drone search began late and covered a limited area, Reeb’s timeline left out evidence from Ashley’s phone). One clip: the final 12 minutes of testimony. Three questions, including one that has students do the arithmetic. Reports do not give the exact minute of the shooting, and the station says so.

## Playback speed

Every clip plays at **1.25×** except the 911 call (Station 9) and the body camera (Station 10), which play at normal speed because the tone and cadence matter there. The notes students see say so. Each clip’s window label shows its watching time at speed (for example “Watch 35:00 to 50:00 · about 12 min at 1.25×”), and a note under the player says what speed it is playing at.

YouTube’s plain embed cannot be set to a speed, so when a student presses play the site loads YouTube’s player script from youtube.com at that moment, creates the player from YouTube’s privacy-enhanced address, and sets the speed. If that script cannot load (a blocked network, for example), a plain player appears instead with a note telling the student to set the speed by hand with the gear icon (Settings, then Playback speed). Students can also change the speed themselves at any time.

To change one clip’s speed, add `rate:1` (normal), `rate:1.5`, or another YouTube speed to that clip’s entry in `index.html`. A clip with no `rate` plays at 1.25×.

## Accuracy notes

Facts come from reporting by CNN, ABC News, CBS News, NBC News, the Dayton Daily News and affiliates, WHIO, WLWT, NewsNation, Court TV, ChurchLeaders and Courtroom Insider, and Ohio law as cited in appellate opinions.

- **Trial status.** Your assignments describe the trial as ongoing. It has finished: jury selection began Sept. 17, 2026, openings were Sept. 18, Botner testified Sept. 23 and 24, the state and defense rested Sept. 28, closings and the verdict were Sept. 29, and sentencing was Oct. 5. The site is built so students decide first and see the outcome last.
- **Botner’s testimony.** She is 24 and testified for the state for almost seven hours. The state said she and Flynn exchanged about 107,000 messages. Defense attorney Emily Smith conducted the cross-examination. Botner has not been charged and testified she was promised nothing. Her own attorney was in the courtroom.
- **Where reports differ.** Outlets give her age when they met as 20 or 21, and give different dates for when the relationship became more personal (2022 versus 2024) and when it became sexual (March versus May 2024). The site says “in 2022,” “spring 2024,” and “reports say she was 20 or 21,” and the question asks students to focus on the sequence. Some outlets quote Flynn’s remark the morning after the shooting as “the way I thought it would” and others “the way we thought it would,” so the site paraphrases it.
- **The texts, the voicemail and the crying.** Message wording and dates come from CNN, ABC News, WBBH/Hearst, WLWT and Court TV, and from an unofficial working transcription of the live stream (justicecaseanalysis.com) that states it contains recognition errors. Messages were chosen where at least one news outlet corroborates the wording, except a few from the transcription alone (February 11, 2025 and August 19, 2025), which the site shortens and attributes to what she read in court. The voicemail is described by WLWT (jurors heard a recorded message in which Flynn apologized for the cruise). Her crying on September 24 while reading her own July 10, 2025 message is reported by ABC News, CNN, TMZ, Mediaite and ChurchLeaders.
- **The police-theory timeline.** The device times come from testimony by BCI analyst Samantha Reeb and digital forensics agent Joseph Wilhelm as reported by WLWT (final outgoing message at 12:45 a.m.; GroupMe and Instagram uninstalled between 1:01 and 1:02; phone plugged in at 1:06 and locked at 1:07; watch steps at 1:19, 1:22, 1:23, between 1:52 and 1:56, and 105 steps between 2:18 and 2:28; the 911 call at about 2:31), with Flynn’s own account from the Dayton Daily News. The first officer’s arrival at about 2:36 and the drone search at about 3:50 come from earlier testimony as reported by the Dayton Daily News. The “How long?” numbers are simple subtraction from those times. Some outlets give the 911 call as “about 2:30” and some say certain apps were removed the day before, so the site says “about” and uses WLWT’s reported times. The exact minute of the shooting is not given in the reports I found. The statement that the state did not have to show where the gun went is a point of law, not a quotation from testimony.
- **The breakup and February 14 to 16.** Message wording and dates come mainly from the unofficial working transcription of the live stream (justicecaseanalysis.com), which states it contains recognition errors, checked against CNN, ABC News, WLWT and Court TV where they report the same message. Quotes are shortened to under 15 words. The events of February 16 (her message “Were you behind this?”, his silence, the couch account, the lie to the FBI, the searches) come from ABC News, CNN and Court TV. The two reports of what he told her when they met in person differ (“the way we thought it would” versus “the way I thought it would”), so the site paraphrases. The detail that Detective Farkas located her at her mother’s house comes from the Springfield News-Sun. The unofficial transcript ends in the middle of February 15, so the site does not quote anything after that from it.
- **The September 29, 2025 moment.** CNN’s live coverage reports that on the stand Botner “paused and whispered, ‘I’m so sorry’” and captions the video “Botner sighs and says ‘I’m so so sorry’ before reading text with vulgar nickname for Ashley Flynn.” The unofficial transcript notes she was embarrassed to read it. I found no report of tears at that moment (the reports of crying are about her July 10 message), so the site describes the pause, sigh and whispered apology and does not say she cried. The nickname is masked as “Ash-s***”; the transcript shows only a partial rendering, so the site quotes just the nickname and says she wrote “have fun with” Ashley. The site no longer links to CNN’s coverage of this moment, so it appears only in the Station 13 text timeline.
- **The Chipotle, tattoo and truck details** come from WLWT, the Dayton Daily News, ChurchLeaders, CNN and ABC News. The detail that Flynn once showed her a gun in his truck’s center console is reported by the Dayton Daily News and is presented as her testimony, with a note that the case involved a missing weapon. It is not presented as the murder weapon.
- **Counts.** Some outlets say 11 charges. Two misdemeanor intimidation counts were dismissed before the verdict. The jury returned verdicts on nine counts.
- **Deliberation length.** Reports range from about two hours to two and a half hours. The site says so.
- **Reasonable doubt.** Ohio’s statute (R.C. 2901.05) says jurors must be firmly convinced of “the truth of the charge.” Your assignment quotes it as “the defendant’s guilt.” The site uses the statute’s wording.
- **Expert witnesses.** The BCI crime-scene agent, the BCI firearms examiner, and the digital forensic examiners gave opinions based on specialized training. The site calls them expert-type witnesses and does not claim anything about how each was formally qualified.
- **Ohio rules cited:** R.C. 2901.05; 2903.01; 2903.02; 2903.11; 2921.12; Ohio Evid.R. 401, 403, 602, 607, 611, 701, 702, 801(D)(2), 901; State v. Jenks, 61 Ohio St.3d 259 (1991); Griffin v. California (1965).

## Moving progress to another device

Progress normally stays in the browser it was typed in. A student who switches computers (or whose Chromebook is swapped) can carry it over without any account or server:

1. **On the old device:** open *My notebook*, then *Move my progress to another device*. Press **Download progress file** or **Copy progress code**.
2. Get it to the new device by emailing it to themselves or saving it in Google Drive.
3. **On the new device:** on the cover, press **Using a new device? Load my saved progress** (or use *My notebook*, then *Move my progress*). Choose the file or paste the code, check the summary, and press **Combine and load**.

Loading *combines* with anything already on that device. For each question the longer answer is kept, so nothing a student has written can be overwritten, and the student’s working mode on that device is kept if they had already started. The summary shows the name, how many responses are in the file, and warns if the name differs from the one on the device.

- The file or code holds the student’s name and answers as plain data, so students should send it only to themselves. It carries a checksum, so a code that was cut off or edited is refused with a clear message, and a code from a different site is refused.
- It works across browsers and devices. A code that wraps across lines when pasted still loads.
- A student who forgot to export can still finish on the original device: the Word notebooks are the turn-in format and can be downloaded at any time.
- Nothing is sent over the internet at any point. This is a transfer the student does by hand.

## Updating the site without losing student progress

Progress is saved in each student’s browser under one key (`CFG.key`, near the top of `index.html`). This version carries over saves from earlier versions of the site automatically: the first time a student opens the new page, any earlier save is merged into the current one. Their name, working mode, quick-check answers, and every response they typed are kept, and if a prompt exists in two saves the longer text wins, so nothing a student wrote is lost. The old save is left in place as a backup.

To keep this working:

- **Upload the new `index.html` to the same repository, with the same name, so the web address does not change.** Progress belongs to the address, so a new address or a renamed repository starts every student from zero.
- **Do not change `CFG.key`, and do not change the `id` of an existing station or question.** Responses are stored by question id. Adding new stations is safe. Removing a station leaves its old responses in the browser but they no longer appear in notebooks.
- Students must use the same browser and device. Clearing browser data erases progress.
- Day 1 is unchanged by this update, so a student who has started or finished Day 1 sees all of it exactly as they left it.
- Several stations changed days or were merged or removed over this project’s updates (the family-witness cross to Day 2; the relationship and ultimatums stations removed; the defense-rests and evidence-ledger stations merged into the closings and verdict stations). Students keep any answers they already wrote in their browsers, but answers to stations that no longer exist do not appear in notebooks, and a student who had finished a day may now see more to do there.
- Questions that were removed (two in Station 8 and the Respond question in Station 7) keep any answer a student already wrote in their browser, but it no longer appears in notebooks. A student who had finished Day 2 will now see two new questions in Station 9.
- “Downloaded” markers from earlier versions are reset (the notebook can simply be downloaded again).

## Publishing on GitHub Pages

1. Create a repository and upload `index.html` and `.nojekyll`.
2. In the repository, open **Settings, then Pages**, choose the **main** branch and the **/ (root)** folder, and save.
3. After a minute the site is live at `https://YOUR-NAME.github.io/REPOSITORY-NAME/`. Add `#day2` (and so on) to a link to send students straight to a day.

Do not rename the repository after students begin, because their saved progress is tied to the web address.

## Privacy

No accounts, no tracking, and no outside services except YouTube (its player script and the video) when a student clicks play. Responses are stored only in the student’s own browser. Students turn work in by downloading the Word notebook and uploading it to Google Classroom.
